WHO

Working-age Malaysians (15–64), with a focus on youth (15–24), women, gig workers, and low-wage earners across 16 states.

WHAT

Labour force participation, unemployment rates, employment status, wage levels, and sectoral distribution from 2021–2026.

WHY

To reveal structural inequalities — who gets left behind, where the decent jobs are, and whether economic growth is reaching ordinary workers.

HOW

Using Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) Labour Force Survey microdata, visualised through 13 custom charts and an interactive choropleth map.

01

The Participation Picture

Malaysia's labour force has broadly recovered post-pandemic, but beneath the headline numbers, participation gaps by gender and age persist — revealing who the economy is actually working for.

Chart 1 — Malaysia's Growing Labour Force, 2021-2026
↗ Malaysia's labour force grew by 1.3 million people over five years — from 16.0M in early 2021 to 17.3M by March 2026 — June 2021 lockdown briefly pulled 33,000 workers out of the market, and a sharp paper drop in January 2024 was simply a government population recount, not real job losses.
Chart 2 — Labour Market Position by State
⊙ Each dot is a state. Right = more people working, up = harder to find a job. The best position is bottom-right — high participation, low unemployment. W.P. Putrajaya sits there alone (78.6%, 0.8%). Sabah is the outlier top-right — people are actively looking but still can't find work (70.1%, 7.5%). Kelantan bottom-left signals many have stopped looking altogether (57.3%).
02

The Unemployment Divide

Malaysia's overall unemployment may look modest at 3.4%, but youth unemployment runs more than three times higher — and for those who lose work, re-entry can take months.

Chart 3 — Unemployment Rate Trend
falling from 4.9% in January 2021 down to 3.1% by December 2024. The recovery moved in three distinct phases: a slow grind downward through early 2022, a rapid decline once borders reopened mid-2022, and then a new stable range that has held ever since.
Chart 4 — Youth vs National Unemployment
Youth unemployment has stayed roughly twice the national rate throughout 2021–2025 — falling from 9.3% to 6.2% for young people, while the national rate dropped from 4.9% to 2.9%. Both lines trend down together, but the gap never closes. This is a structural divide, not a pandemic hangover.
Chart 5 — Duration of Unemployment
⟳ Over 40% of unemployed remain jobless for 3+ months, rising to 6+ months for graduates — a signal of skills mismatch, not laziness.
Chart 6 — Unemployment Rate by Age Group
The 15–19 and 20–24 age groups posted the strongest gains (up ~47% and ~37% respectively from 2021–2024), largely driven by minimum wage hikes. Yet the absolute gap remains stark: a 20–24 year old earning RM 2,255 still makes less than half of what a 45–49 year old takes home at RM 4,517.
Chart 7 — Choropleth Map: Unemployment Rate by State

03

How Malaysians Work

The nature of employment is shifting. Formal employment grows, but so does the army of own-account workers and unpaid family helpers — a precarious informal economy running alongside the formal one.

Chart 8 — Employment by Sector
⊛ Services dominate at 62% of total employment. Agriculture continues to shrink but remains the backbone of rural East Malaysia.
Chart 9 — Employment by Status
The share of employees (salaried workers) has been gradually declining, while own-account workers are on the rise — a quiet but meaningful structural shift in how Malaysians earn a living. Employers and unpaid family workers remain relatively flat. The trend suggests more Malaysians are moving into informal or self-directed work, whether by choice or necessity, rather than taking up conventional wage employment.
04

The Wage Gap

Wages are rising — but not equally. The gap between high-skill sectors like finance and low-wage sectors like agriculture is widening. And for many workers, wages still fall below a living wage.

Chart 10 — Median Wage by Sector Over Time
**Minimum wage hikes are lifting the floor — but the youngest workers are barely clearing it.** The 15–19 age group's median wage sat below the minimum wage threshold in earlier years, highlighted in red, only catching up as the floor rose. Workers aged 25 and above consistently earn well above the minimum, with wages peaking in the 40–54 range. The chart tells two stories at once: older workers enjoy a comfortable buffer above the legal minimum, while teenagers and young adults are essentially minimum wage workers with little room above the threshold.
Data Sources

Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) — Labour Force Survey (LFS) 2019–2024. Employment Statistics, Wages & Salaries Statistics. data.gov.my open data portal.

Methodology

Monthly LFS microdata aggregated by sex, state, age group, employment status, and occupation. Wages deflated to 2020 constant prices using CPI.
